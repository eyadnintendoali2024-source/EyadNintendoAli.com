PAGE 01 — THE PALEOLITHIC AGE
Welcome, everyone. Today our smart screen takes us far back in time to the Paleolithic Age, also called the Old Stone Age. We will learn how early humans found food, made tools, used fire, created art, and adapted to a changing world.

PAGE 02 — WHAT DOES PALEOLITHIC MEAN?
The word Paleolithic comes from Greek words meaning old stone. It describes a very long part of human history, before farming and cities. A key ability was making tools from stone and other natural materials. Think about how a simple tool could change daily life.

PAGE 03 — FINDING FOOD
Early humans gathered nuts, berries, fruits, and green plants. They also hunted animals such as buffalo, horses, bison, and reindeer. Near coasts and rivers, people could catch fish and gather shellfish. Finding food meant observing seasons, plants, animal tracks, and water.

PAGE 04 — FLINT, HAND AXES, AND SPEARS
Look closely at the spear on the screen. First, a person selected a strong piece of wood for the shaft. Next, flint was carefully struck and shaped until it had a sharp point. The stone point was attached firmly to the wooden shaft. This made a spear. A spear helped a hunter reach a large animal from farther away than a hand could reach. Hunters still needed patience, teamwork, and caution because animals could be dangerous. The spear was one of several important stone tools; hand axes were useful for cutting and working materials.

PAGE 05 — LATER INVENTIONS
Over thousands of years, tools became more specialized. A bow stored energy in a bent branch so an arrow could travel toward prey from a distance. Harpoons helped people catch animals in water. Fishhooks helped catch smaller fish, and bone needles could join animal hides to make clothing. Each invention solved a practical problem.

PAGE 06 — NOMADIC LIFE AND SHELTER
Many groups moved from place to place to follow food and seasons. We call a way of life that moves regularly nomadic. Natural shelters included caves and rock overhangs. People also made shelters from poles, branches, and animal hides. Shelter protected people from weather and helped make a safer camp.

PAGE 07 — SHARED WORK
This page describes different tasks people in a group might do, including gathering plants, caring for children, preparing meals, trapping small animals, and traveling to hunt. The important idea is cooperation. People shared food, knowledge, and decisions. The exact jobs could differ between groups and places, but every person’s skills helped the community.

PAGE 08 — ADAPTING TO SURVIVE
Learning to use fire was a major change. Archaeological evidence shows that early humans learned to control fire over a very long time. Fire changed how people could live in cold places and after dark. We will look next at why fire was so useful and how people could make it.

PAGE 09 — THE POWER OF FIRE
Fire provided heat in freezing weather and light after sunset. It helped people cook food, which could make meals safer and easier to eat. A fire near camp could also discourage some dangerous animals. People had to gather fuel and watch the flames carefully so the fire did not spread.

PAGE 10 — HOW PEOPLE STARTED FIRES
One method used friction: people rubbed dry wood together until heat helped create an ember. Another method made sparks by striking suitable stones together. A tiny ember needed dry grass or other tinder and careful blowing before it became a larger flame. Starting a fire took practice, preparation, and patience.

PAGE 11 — FIELD ARCHAEOLOGY
Archaeologists study evidence left by people in the past. Before digging, they may use maps, aerial photographs, satellite images, and GPS to understand a place. A field archaeologist records where objects are found. Careful notes and photographs help researchers understand how people lived.

PAGE 12 — THE ARCHAEOLOGICAL DIG
A dig begins by marking a grid so each find has a known location. Archaeologists remove soil carefully, often a little at a time. They record, photograph, and map each object before moving it. The position of a find can be as important as the object itself. A careless dig could destroy useful evidence.

PAGE 13 — HOW OLD IS IT?
Scientists use different tests to learn about the past. Radiocarbon dating can help date once-living material. Other methods include potassium-argon dating, thermoluminescence, tree-ring study, and DNA analysis. Stratigraphy studies layers of soil: when layers have not been disturbed, deeper layers are often older than the layers above them.

PAGE 14 — CHAUVET CAVE
Chauvet Cave in France contains very old images of animals. The cave paintings show that people carefully observed animals and used images to communicate ideas. Protected caves preserve clues that can survive for thousands of years. A cave wall can be a primary source: direct evidence from the past.

PAGE 15 — SURVIVING THE ICE AGES
During the Ice Ages, huge areas of land were covered by ice, and temperatures could be severe. Early humans adapted by changing how they lived: finding shelter, making clothing, using fire, and planning around local food. The big idea is that survival depended on learning and adapting, not only on physical strength.

PAGE 16 — ICE AGE GEOGRAPHY
When more of Earth’s water was locked in ice, sea levels fell. In some places, land that is now underwater formed a bridge between regions. Animals and people could cross these land bridges and migrate. Geography and climate shaped where people could travel and find food.

PAGE 17 — CREATING CAVE ART
Important cave-art sites include Altamira in Spain and Lascaux and Chauvet in France. Artists painted animals and signs on cave walls. We cannot know every reason they made art. The images may have had spiritual meaning, helped people think about hunting, or simply been made because people wanted to create.

PAGE 18 — HOW CAVE ART WAS MADE
Artists needed light underground, so they used lamps with animal fat. They used fingers, sticks, and other simple tools. Mineral pigments made colors such as red, yellow, and black. Some artists blew pigment through a hollow reed to make outlines or hand stencils. The cave itself became both a working space and a canvas.

PAGE 19 — WHY DID THEY PAINT?
One theory connects cave images with belief or ritual, perhaps hopes for success in hunting. Another theory is that people made art to create something beautiful. These are explanations scholars discuss, not facts we can prove for every painting. We do know that art was part of human life long before writing.

PAGE 20 — THE BLUEPRINT FOR SURVIVAL
Food, technology, environment, adaptation, and culture all connect. People searched for food, made tools, found shelter, and passed knowledge to others. When the environment changed, people adapted their choices. A tool is useful, but knowledge and cooperation help a community use it well.

PAGE 21 — REVIEW AND THINK
Let us review. Two important Paleolithic innovations were tools and the use of fire. Paleolithic means Old Stone Age. Nomadic describes moving from place to place. An artifact is an object made or used by people. Now think: how do people today change their behavior to fit an environment, and how do people also change the environment around them?
